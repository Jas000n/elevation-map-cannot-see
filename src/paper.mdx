## Abstract

Navigation for humanoid robots is critical, yet large-scale evaluation on physical hardware is often impractical due to cost and safety concerns, making simulation benchmarks essential. Existing VLN benchmarks achieve physically executable navigation, but still assume (1) all hazards are observable from elevation maps; (2) realized motions closely match desired motions. In real environments, however, fallen bottles may be ambiguous in elevation maps, while phones and water spills may be difficult to differentiate; hazard avoidance by the locomotion policy can cause the robot's actual trajectory to deviate from the path intended by the VLN policy. Such command-execution mismatch can accumulate and lead the robot toward unintended locations. To expose these failure modes, we introduce a benchmark that models both elevation-subtle hazards and execution deviations, together with a closed-loop VLN + locomotion control framework that continuously realigns high-level navigation with the robot's actual state. We evaluate navigation in simulation and further validate the locomotion policy on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Results show that semantic input reduces contact with hazards poorly represented in elevation maps, while anti-deviation improves navigation success. These findings highlight the need to evaluate humanoid navigation jointly in terms of route completion and hazard avoidance.

## What the elevation map cannot see

Legged robots usually read the ground through an elevation map built from depth or LiDAR. It is very good at boxes. It is much worse at a fallen bottle, and it cannot represent a phone or a water or oil stain at all, although stepping on any of them can end a walk. We group the hazards in our benchmark by how they appear in an elevation map:

| Elevation visibility | Hazard | Height (m) | Footprint (m) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: |

## Method

<Wide>
<Figure>
  <Picture slot="figure" src={fig2} alt="Method overview: a semantic teacher trained on ground-truth elevation and semantic maps, distilled into an RGB-D student; a VLN module whose waypoints are corrected by anti-deviation" />
  <Fragment slot="caption">(a) A semantic-aware teacher learns locomotion from ground-truth elevation and semantic maps. (b) DAgger distils the teacher into an RGB-D student with an elevation branch and a semantic branch. (c) A high-level VLN module (from NaVILA) produces relative target poses; the anti-deviation module tracks them with localization feedback.</Fragment>
</Figure>
</Wide>

The locomotion policy keeps the network of an attention-based map-encoding (AME) policy that already traverses challenging terrain, and adds an independent **semantic branch** next to its elevation branch. The elevation branch encodes the shape of the terrain. The semantic branch adds a steppability prior that marks safe support and hazardous regions. Training has three stages:

1. **Semantic teacher.** Starting from the pretrained AME policy, PPO on challenging terrain with visible, ambiguous and invisible hazards added. The teacher sees ground-truth elevation and semantic maps and is trained with two foot-placement rewards, described below.
2. **Robust teacher.** PPO continues with curriculum randomisation of dynamics, payload, proprioceptive noise, pushes and external forces.
3. **Deployable student.** DAgger distils the teacher into a student that sees only a waist-mounted D435. Depth feeds the elevation branch. RGB, together with features from a frozen DINOv2-S backbone, feeds the semantic branch. On hardware, stereo depth from Fast-FoundationStereo replaces raw depth.

### Rewards for stepping over hazards

<TwoColumns>
  <Figure slot="left">
    <Picture slot="figure" src={fig7} alt="Step-over clearance reward and premature-descent penalty on a swing foot above a spill" />
    <Fragment slot="caption">Top: the clearance reward for a swing foot moving horizontally over a hazard. Bottom: the penalty for lowering it while it still overlaps the hazard.</Fragment>
  </Figure>
  <div slot="right">
    Two terms shape how a foot crosses a hazard: a clearance reward while the swing foot moves over it, and a penalty for lowering the foot before it has passed. Both are active only for a swing foot whose sole footprint overlaps a hazard, and are exactly zero elsewhere, so the nominal gait is untouched.
  </div>
</TwoColumns>

With $d_i = z_i - (h_i + \epsilon)$ the clearance of foot $i$ above the top of the hazard beneath it, $H_i$ the overlap indicator and $g_i$ the swing gate (active only while the robot is commanded to move):

$$
r_{\text{over}} = \frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{i} g_i H_i \tanh\!\big(k\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i,xy}\rVert\big)\Big[\tfrac{d_i}{\delta}\Big]_0^1
$$

$$
p_{\text{desc}} = \frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{i} g_i H_i\, \mathbb{1}[d_i>0]\Big[1-\tfrac{d_i}{D}\Big]_0^1 \tanh\!\Big(\tfrac{\max(-v_{i,z},\,0)}{v_s}\Big)
$$

The horizontal-speed gate keeps a stationary raised foot from collecting clearance reward. The penalty grows as a descending foot nears the hazard; contact itself ends the episode.

### Anti-deviation: closing the loop with navigation

<TwoColumns>
  <Figure slot="left">
    <Picture slot="figure" src={fig8} alt="The robot detours around hazards (green), drifting from the VLN-commanded direction (red); anti-deviation steers it back (blue)" />
    <Fragment slot="caption">Command–execution mismatch. With no safe foothold along the commanded direction (red), the robot detours (green); anti-deviation steers it back toward the intended target pose (blue).</Fragment>
  </Figure>
  <div slot="right">
    Avoiding a hazard takes sidesteps, shorter steps or turns, and those leave the robot somewhere the VLN policy did not plan for. Executed open-loop, each waypoint $w_t$ becomes a constant velocity command, the deviation carries into the next VLN decision, and the error accumulates. Anti-deviation anchors the target pose at decision time, $p_t^{\text{tar}} = p_t \oplus w_t$, estimates the current pose $\hat p_t$ from the same monocular RGB stream (LingBot-Map in simulation), and maps the residual to a compensatory command:

    $$
    d_t = p_t^{\text{tar}} - \hat p_t, \qquad c_t^{\text{comp}} = \kappa_{\text{ad}}(d_t)
    $$

    The compensatory command takes the slot of the velocity command. The locomotion policy keeps its inputs and action interface unchanged.
  </div>
</TwoColumns>

## Locomotion in simulation

The simulated benchmark covers procedurally generated corridors in four terrain groups (uneven ground, stairs, stakes and stonebridge), each at three difficulty levels, with the six hazard types placed along the way. On top of that come perturbations to dynamics, payload, pushes, sensing, lighting, surface colour, camera pose and camera timing, all scaled together; at scale 1.0 the robot is, for example, pushed at up to 0.8 m/s every 3–7 s and carries up to ±2 kg of extra mass. ### Success across difficulty and perturbation

Success means finishing the corridor without leaving it, without falling and without touching any obstacle or hazard. Progress is the fraction of the distance covered before an episode ends. In clean conditions, Ours raises success over Elevation-only by 23.6, 28.5 and 25.8 points on L1, L2 and L3. The gap narrows under the strongest perturbation: at L3 and 1.5×, Ours still covers 56.7% of the distance but completes only 22.7% of episodes. ### Where the semantic branch helps

On uneven ground, Ours succeeds in 50.6% of clean episodes against 9.4% for Elevation-only. The improvement comes from hazards the elevation map cannot show. ## Limitations

- **The gap to the teacher remains.** On clean uneven ground, Ours succeeds in 50.6% of episodes against the ground-truth-map Oracle's 93.6%. Its contact rate on *visible* hazards is 24.7% against 2.2%, so handling ambiguous and invisible hazards alone does not make a route reliable. Because the two differ in both observations and training role, these results do not separate perception error from distillation or control limits.
- **Falls under strong perturbation.** At 1.5× perturbation the fall rate of Ours rises to 11.9%, against 5.8% for the Oracle and 3.1% for Elevation-only, possibly because the camera is sensitive to lighting changes.
- **Memory and hardware.** The waist camera sees only what is in front of the feet. Persistent semantic mapping could keep hazards that have left the field of view. The navigation framework has so far been validated only in simulation.
